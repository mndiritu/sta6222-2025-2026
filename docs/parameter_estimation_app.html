Parameter Estimation & Model Fitting

Comprehensive "Ground Zero" Study Guide | STA 6222 | Dr. Moses Ndiritu

1. Why Parameter Estimation Matters

A transmission model is only as useful as its parameters. In real outbreaks, we do not know the true transmission rate, reporting fraction, or even the initial number of infectious individuals. Estimation links surveillance data to model-based decisions like forecasting, intervention planning, and uncertainty assessment.

Key Idea

You are not fitting "the model" to data directly. You are fitting model predictions to noisy observations using an explicit Observation Model (how the data are generated).

Process vs. Observation Model

It is crucial to distinguish between the biology and the data:

  • Process Model (Biology): The compartment states ($S, E, I, R$) that evolve via differential equations. This is the "hidden truth."
  • Observation Model (Data): The filtered, delayed, and incomplete view of those states (e.g., Reported Cases = 30% of True Infections).

Data Types & Issues

Incidence

New cases per time step (daily/weekly). Most common, but affected by reporting delays and case definitions.

Prevalence

Number currently infected at time $t$. Often hard to measure directly.

Seroprevalence

Cumulative exposure (antibodies). Useful for final size ($Z$) and under-ascertainment.

Deaths / Hospitalisations

Delayed signals, sometimes more reliable than case counts.

2. Understanding the Parameters

In ODE models, parameters are usually rates (events per time unit), not probabilities.

  • $\beta$ (Transmission Rate): The rate at which an infected individual generates new infections per unit time.
    Units: $time^{-1}$. Formula: $\beta = c \times p$ (contact rate $\times$ prob. of transmission).
  • $\gamma$ (Recovery Rate): The rate at which individuals leave the infectious class.
    Units: $time^{-1}$. Relationship: $\gamma = 1 / D$, where $D$ is the mean infectious period.
  • $\sigma$ (Incubation Rate): The rate at which exposed individuals become infectious.
    Units: $time^{-1}$. Relationship: $\sigma = 1 / L$, where $L$ is the mean latent period.

3. Early Exponential Growth

When susceptibles are not yet depleted ($S \approx 1$) and behavior is stable, the epidemic grows exponentially:

$$I(t) \approx I_0 e^{rt}$$

Here, $r$ is the observed growth rate. It represents the slope of the epidemic curve on a logarithmic scale.

Linking Growth Rate to Doubling Time

$$T_d = \frac{\ln(2)}{r} \approx \frac{0.693}{r}$$

Practical Warning: Exponential growth is a short-window approximation. Once behavior changes, testing expands, or susceptibles are depleted, this approximation breaks.

4. Relating $r$ to $R_0$

We observe $r$ from data, but we need $R_0$ to understand severity. The link depends on the generation interval.

SIR Approximation

Assumes no latent period. $r \approx \beta - \gamma$.

$$R_0 = 1 + \frac{r}{\gamma}$$

SEIR Approximation

Includes latent period ($1/\sigma$). Delays transmission.

$$R_0 = (1 + \frac{r}{\sigma})(1 + \frac{r}{\gamma})$$

Using the Generation Interval Distribution $w(t)$

In general, $R_0$ cannot be determined from $r$ alone without assumptions about the generation-interval distribution $w(t)$. A common relationship (Euler-Lotka type) is:

$$R_0 = \frac{1}{\int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-rt} w(t) dt}$$
Interpretation: For the same observed growth $r$, a shorter generation interval implies a smaller $R_0$, while a longer generation interval implies a larger $R_0$.

5. Estimation Workflows

A. Quick Estimation (GLM)

To estimate $r$ from early incidence counts, use a Poisson GLM ($log(\lambda_t) = a + rt$), not linear regression.

  • Window Selection: Choose the period before major interventions, before strong testing expansion, and before obvious depletion of susceptibles. Document your choice.
  • Overdispersion: If Variance $\gg$ Mean, prefer Negative Binomial GLM to avoid false certainty (confidence intervals too narrow).

B. Full Trajectory Fitting (Likelihood-based)

Rather than fitting a single summary ($r$), you can fit the full model trajectory to data.

  1. Choose Process Model: (e.g., SIR or SEIR) with parameters $\theta$.
  2. Solve Numerically: Generate model states over time ($S(t), I(t)$).
  3. Link to Observation: Define likelihood linking states to observed data (Poisson/NB counts, reporting fraction, delay).
  4. Estimate: Maximize likelihood (MLE) or use Bayesian inference (posterior) to find best $\theta$.
  5. Validate: Check residuals, predictive checks, and sensitivity analysis.

Overdispersion Note: Real surveillance data are often more variable than Poisson. Negative Binomial likelihoods are common and usually more realistic.

C. Final Size Equation (SIR)

If you can estimate the final attack rate $Z$ (cumulative proportion infected) for a largely closed epidemic, the SIR final size equation links $Z$ to $R_0$:

$$Z = 1 - e^{-R_0 Z}$$

Best for a single-wave epidemic with relatively stable parameters and reasonable measurement of cumulative infection (e.g., via serology).

6. Practical Pitfalls & Identifiability

Identifiability: Can you distinguish between a high transmission rate and a high reporting rate? Reporting fraction and transmission can trade off (you can fit cases by changing either).

  • • Initial Conditions: An incorrect $I_0$ can significantly bias estimates of $\beta$ and $R_0$.
  • • Delays: Incubation and reporting delays shift peaks and can mislead fitting if ignored.
  • • Time-Varying Parameters: Interventions change parameters over time. A single constant $\beta$ may be wrong.
  • • Heterogeneity: Spatial, age, or network heterogeneity means the "average" parameter might not represent the dynamics well.
Good Practice: State your model, your observation process, your fitting method, and your assumptions. Then report uncertainty (confidence intervals or credible intervals).
