2. Week 11: Advanced Structure (Patches & Networks)

2.1 Metapopulation Models

Populations are split into \(m\) patches. Infection pressure is not just local; it arrives from other patches via movement or mixing.

The Force of Infection equation:

$$ \lambda_{i}(t) = \sum_{j} \beta_{ij} \left( \frac{I_j}{N_j} \right) $$

Interpretation: The risk to patch \(i\) is the sum of risks from all patches \(j\), weighted by the coupling \(\beta_{ij}\). In Kenya, think of Nairobi as a hub patch fed by commuters from Kiambu and Machakos.

2.2 Network Models

Instead of assuming everyone mixes randomly, network models account for contacts (degree \(k\)). Some people have many contacts (hubs), while others have few.

Definition of \(R_n\) (Network Reproduction Number):

Unlike the standard \(R_0\), the threshold for an outbreak in a network depends on the variance of contacts:

$$ R_{n} \propto \frac{E[k^2] - E[k]}{E[k]} $$

Why is this important?

  • The "Hub" Effect: \(E[k^2]\) is the "second moment" of the degree distribution. If there is high variance (some people are "superspreaders" with huge \(k\)), \(E[k^2]\) becomes very large.
  • Outbreak Risk: A high \(R_n\) means outbreaks are easier to sustain even if the average number of contacts is low. One busy market trader or matatu conductor can keep the transmission chain alive.
  • Policy Implication: Targeted interventions (e.g., vaccinating the 10% most connected people) are far more effective than random vaccination in these settings.

2.3 Co-infection

Example: HIV and TB. We model this with compartments like \(I_A\), \(I_B\), and \(I_{AB}\).

Interaction Term: If infection A makes you \(q\) times more susceptible to B, the flow from \(I_A\) to \(I_{AB}\) is \( q \cdot \lambda_B \cdot I_A \).

3. Week 12: Policy & Management

3.1 Vaccine Threshold Calculation

To control an epidemic, we need \(R_e < 1\). With a vaccine of efficacy \(e\) and coverage \(p\), the susceptible fraction becomes \(1 - p\cdot e\).

Derivation:

$$ R_0(1 - p \cdot e) < 1 \implies p > \frac{1 - 1/R_0}{e} $$

Interpretation: If efficacy \(e < 1\), you need higher coverage than the standard herd immunity threshold.

4. Week 13: Statistical Inference

4.1 Poisson Likelihood

This connects the model (theory) to data (observations). If cases \(y_t\) follow a Poisson process with mean modeled by \(\lambda_t(\theta)\):

$$ l(\theta) = \sum_t [ y_t \log(\lambda_t(\theta)) - \lambda_t(\theta) ] + \text{const} $$

We maximize this to find the best parameters.

4.2 Overdispersion (Negative Binomial)

Real surveillance data often varies more than the Poisson assumption (Variance > Mean). We use the Negative Binomial distribution where:

$$ Var(Y) = \mu + \frac{\mu^2}{k} $$

As \(k \to \infty\), it looks like Poisson. Small \(k\) implies strong clustering or superspreading.

4.3 Bayesian Inference & MCMC (Metropolis-Hastings)

Bayes Rule (Concept):

$$ p(\theta | \text{data}) \propto L(\text{data} | \theta) \cdot p(\theta) $$

The Posterior is your belief after seeing data. Credible Intervals summarize this uncertainty (e.g., "95% probability the true R0 is between 2.1 and 2.5").

Metropolis-Hastings Intuition:

Since the posterior is often impossible to calculate directly, we "explore" it using a random walk:

  1. Propose a new parameter \(\theta'\) near the current \(\theta\).
  2. Compare: Does \(\theta'\) explain the data better than \(\theta\)?
  3. Accept/Reject: Accept \(\theta'\) with probability \(\alpha\). If accepted, move there; if not, stay at \(\theta\).
  4. Repeat: The resulting chain of samples approximates the posterior distribution.

Diagnostics (Exam Keywords): Trace plots (should look like "fuzzy caterpillars"), R-hat (should be < 1.05), Effective Sample Size (ESS).

Self-Study Quiz: Problem Set

Test your understanding of the Problem Set. Click "Reveal Answer" to check your reasoning against the solution key.

Q1. Metapopulation: If Patch A has an outbreak and B has none, name two mechanisms by which B can eventually get infected.

Mechanisms:

  1. Importation/Movement: Infected travelers/commuters move from A to B.
  2. Coupling/Mixing: Individuals in B have contact with individuals in A without permanently moving (force of infection coupling \(\beta_{BA}\)).
Q2. Calculate the force of infection \(\lambda_1\) if \(I_1/N_1=0.01, I_2/N_2=0.03\), and coupling is \(b_{11}=0.6, b_{12}=0.2, b_{13}=0.1\).

Calculation:

$$ \lambda_1 = \sum b_{1j}(I_j/N_j) = 0.6(0.01) + 0.2(0.03) + 0.1(0.00) $$ $$ \lambda_1 = 0.006 + 0.006 = 0.012 \text{ per week} $$

Interpretation: Patch 1 experiences a 1.2% per-week infection pressure from combined local and external sources.

Q3. Two communities have the same mean degree \(k\). One has high variance. Which is more outbreak-prone?

The community with high variance.

Reasoning: \(R_n \propto (E[k^2] - E[k])/E[k]\). Higher variance implies a much larger \(E[k^2]\) (second moment), which increases \(R_n\) due to "hub-driven" spread.

Q6. Policy Threshold. If \(R_n = 2.5\) and Vaccine Efficacy \(e = 0.75\), what is the minimum coverage \(p\) needed?

Formula: \( p > (1 - 1/R_n)/e \)

Step 1: Herd immunity threshold = \( 1 - 1/2.5 = 1 - 0.4 = 0.6 \).

Step 2: Adjust for efficacy: \( 0.6 / 0.75 = 0.8 \).

Result: You need 80% coverage.

Q8. What does a small \(k\) imply in Negative Binomial surveillance data?

Strong Overdispersion/Clustering.

As \(k \to \infty\), variance approaches the mean (Poisson). Small \(k\) means the variance is much larger than the mean (\(Var = \mu + \mu^2/k\)), implying superspreading events or heterogeneous reporting.

Week 11: Metapopulations & Kenya

Kenya is not "one mixing pot". It is patches connected by movement.

Force of Infection: \( \lambda_i = \sum_j \beta_{ij} (I_j/N_j) \)

Travel Restrictions: They reduce \(\beta_{ij}\) (importation). If local \(R_e > 1\), they delay outbreaks but do not necessarily prevent them.

Week 11: Networks & Superspreading

Hubs: High degree nodes (matatu stages, markets) drive spread.

Key Intuition: \( R_n \propto E[K^2] \). High variance in contacts = High Risk.

Policy: Targeted interventions (vaccinating hubs) outperform uniform random strategies.

Week 12: Policy Thresholds

Vaccine Threshold Derivation:

Control requires \( R_0(1 - p \cdot e) < 1 \)

\( p > \frac{1 - 1/R_0}{e} \)

Kenyan Scenario: Choosing between Intervention A (reliable, low impact) vs Intervention B (high impact, uncertain uptake).
Exam Tip: Prefer robust options when uncertainty is high.

Week 13: Likelihood & Overdispersion

Poisson: Assumes Variance = Mean. Often too "tight" for real data.

Negative Binomial: \( Var = \mu + \mu^2/k \). Handles clustering/superspreading.

Identifiability: \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) can trade off. You might need external data (like serology) to separate them.

Week 13: Bayesian Intuition

Bayes Rule: \( p(\theta|data) \propto L(data|\theta) \cdot p(\theta) \)

MCMC (Metropolis-Hastings): A random walk that spends more time where the posterior probability is high.

Why? Allows us to quantify uncertainty (Credible Intervals) for policy makers.
